Perplexity measures the effective number of possible states of a system, accounting for their probabilities. Entropy is perplexity in log form, which makes it additive.

$$
H(X) = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} p(x) \log p(x) , \tag{1}
$$

where $$X$$ is a random variable on a finite alphabet $$\mathcal{X}$$ with distribution $$p(x) = \mathbb{P}(X = x)$$, and $$\log$$ means $$\log_2$$, so entropy is measured in bits.


Entropy describes a system asymptotically: as the number of observations $$n \to \infty$$, the system effectively has about $$2^{nH}$$ equally likely outcome sequences, so

$$
H = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{n} \log_2 \left| A_\varepsilon^{(n)} \right|
$$

is the log-count of possible states per observation in the limit, where $$A_\varepsilon^{(n)}$$ is the set of typical sequences of length $$n$$.

Because entropy is an asymptotic limit, compressing data to near its entropy requires coding long sequences jointly, either explicitly through blocking or implicitly through arithmetic coding. Both spread the integer-bit overhead across many symbols, and when paired with a context model, they also exploit dependencies between symbols.


Entropy can be added, subtracted, and compared like mass or energy. Some useful equations:

Two variables at once have a single joint distribution $$p(x, y) = \mathbb{P}(X = x, Y = y)$$ on $$\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$$, and its joint entropy obeys a chain rule:

$$
\begin{align*}
H(X, Y) &= -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} p(x, y) \log p(x, y) \tag{2} \\[4pt]
&= H(X) + H(Y \mid X) \;=\; H(Y) + H(X \mid Y) \tag{3}
\end{align*}
$$

Mutual information:

$$
\begin{align*}
I(X; Y) &= H(X) - H(X \mid Y) \\[4pt]
&= H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) \\[4pt]
&= H(X) + H(Y) - H(X, Y)
\end{align*}
$$

In particular it is symmetric, and $$I(X; X) = H(X)$$ — a variable tells you everything about itself. That identity is why $$H(X)$$ is sometimes read as a variable's *self-information*, though the term more often names the per-outcome surprisal $$-\log p(x)$$, whose average is (1).

Mutual information is also the quantity the noisy-channel coding theorem turns on. Every discrete memoryless channel has a capacity $$C = \max_{p(x)} I(X; Y)$$, and data can be sent with arbitrarily small error at any rate below $$C$$, but at no rate above it, with the guarantee holding asymptotically for long codes.

Cross-entropy measures the cost of believing the wrong distribution. If the data really comes from $$p$$ but you encode it with a code built for $$q$$, every symbol costs $$-\log q(x)$$ bits instead of $$-\log p(x)$$, and the average bill is

$$
\begin{align*}
H(p \parallel q) &= -\sum_x p(x) \log q(x) \\[4pt]
&= -\sum_x p(x) \log p(x) \;+\; \sum_x p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \\[4pt]
&= H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \parallel q) . \tag{4}
\end{align*}
$$

Consider an example. Let the true distribution of a die be $$p = (1/3, 1/3, 1/3, 0, 0, 0)$$, while the model is confident it is almost always a six: $$q = (0.02, 0.02, 0.02, 0.02, 0.02, 0.9)$$.

$$
\begin{align*}
H(q) &\approx 0.70 \text{ bits}, &&2^{H(q)} \approx 1.6 \\[4pt]
H(p \parallel q) &= -\log 0.02 \approx 5.64 \text{ bits}, &&2^{H(p \parallel q)} = 50 \\[4pt]
H(p) &= \log 3 \approx 1.58 \text{ bits}, &&D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \parallel q) \approx 4.06 \text{ bits}
\end{align*}
$$

$$H(p)$$ is how uncertain the source really is. $$H(q)$$ is how uncertain the model *believes* it is, and it never touches reality at all — it is the model grading its own confidence. Only $$H(p \parallel q)$$ lets reality choose the outcomes and the model pay for them: $$p$$ weights, $$q$$ is charged. So $$2^{H(q)}$$ is the number of states the model thinks it faces, while $$2^{H(p \parallel q)}$$ is the number it *performs* like once the data arrives. Reporting the first as though it were the second is the easy mistake. Here the model rates itself at fewer than two effective states and then performs like a guess among fifty, because every outcome that actually occurs is one it gave a 2 per cent chance; the 4.06 bits of surcharge is what that confident error costs.
