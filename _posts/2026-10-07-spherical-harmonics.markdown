Laplace's equation $$\nabla^2 f = 0$$ asks for a function with no sources and no sinks — one whose value at every point is the average of its neighbours. In three dimensions, for a function $$f(x,y,z)$$, it is a statement about three second derivatives cancelling:

$$
\nabla^2 f = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial z^2} = 0 . \tag{1}
$$

It describes a gravitational or electrostatic potential in empty space, a steady temperature, the flow of an incompressible fluid. Spherical harmonics are what drops out when that equation is solved in spherical coordinates, by separating the variables one at a time.

### The equation in spherical coordinates

Spherical coordinates place a point at distance $$r$$ from the origin, at polar angle $$\theta$$ measured down from the $$z$$-axis, and azimuth $$\varphi$$ measured round from the $$x$$-axis:

$$
x = r\sin\theta\cos\varphi , \qquad y = r\sin\theta\sin\varphi , \qquad z = r\cos\theta ,
$$

with $$r \ge 0$$, $$\theta \in [0,\pi]$$ and $$\varphi \in [0,2\pi)$$. Conventions differ over which angle gets which letter; this is the one physics uses, and it makes $$\theta = 0$$ and $$\theta = \pi$$ the two poles.

Equation (2) is less arbitrary than it looks once you read it as a statement about that box. The Laplacian is $$\nabla\cdot\nabla f$$ — the net outward flux of $$\nabla f$$ per unit volume — so all three terms are the same bookkeeping applied to three pairs of faces, and every factor in them is a length or an area taken from the figure.

What matters about this shape is that $$r$$ appears only as the overall factor $$1/r^2$$ on the two angular terms. That is exactly what makes the next step possible.

### Separating radius from angle

It pays to name the angular half of (2) before splitting anything. Both angular terms carry the same factor $$1/r^2$$; strip it off and what is left differentiates only $$\theta$$ and $$\varphi$$,

$$
\Delta_{S^2} \;=\; \frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right)
\;+\; \frac{1}{\sin^2\!\theta}\,\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\varphi^2} ,
$$

so that (2) reads simply

$$
\nabla^2 f = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r^2\frac{\partial f}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2}\,\Delta_{S^2} f .
$$

This $$\Delta_{S^2}$$ is the Laplacian of the unit sphere itself. The subscript is a name rather than an exponent: $$S^2$$ denotes the two-dimensional unit sphere, $$S$$ for sphere and the $$2$$ for its dimension, since the two angles $$\theta$$ and $$\varphi$$ between them fix a point on it. Nothing is being squared, and the surface counts as two-dimensional even though it sits in three-dimensional space.

Now look for a solution of the form $$f(r,\theta,\varphi) = R(r)\,Y(\theta,\varphi)$$. Substituting into (2) and multiplying through by $$r^2/(RY)$$ gathers each variable on its own side:

$$
\frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2 \frac{dR}{dr}\right) \;=\; -\frac{1}{Y}\,\Delta_{S^2} Y , \tag{3}
$$

The left side cannot depend on the angles and the right side cannot depend on $$r$$, so both equal the same constant. That is the entire method, and it gets used once more below.

Call that constant $$\lambda$$ for the moment, making no assumption about it. The radial half of (3) is then

$$
\frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) = \lambda ,
$$

and expanding the derivative and multiplying through by $$R$$ leaves an ordinary differential equation:

$$
r^2 R'' + 2rR' - \lambda R = 0 .
$$

This is an Euler equation — in every term the power of $$r$$ matches the order of the derivative — and such equations invite powers of $$r$$ as trial solutions, since differentiating one only lowers its exponent by as much as the accompanying $$r$$ raises it. Substituting $$R = r^s$$ and dividing by $$r^s$$:

$$
s(s-1) + 2s - \lambda = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad s(s+1) = \lambda .
$$

Only now is there a reason to name $$\lambda$$ differently. Writing $$\lambda = l(l+1)$$ makes the equation $$s(s+1) = l(l+1)$$, whose roots can be read off without the quadratic formula: $$s = l$$ and $$s = -(l+1)$$. It is a relabelling and nothing more at this stage; that $$l$$ must be a non-negative integer is something the angles will force later. So

$$
R(r) = A\,r^{\,l} + \frac{B}{r^{\,l+1}} . \tag{4}
$$

The angular side is left as an eigenvalue problem on the unit sphere:

$$
\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial Y}{\partial\theta}\right)
+ \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial\varphi^2} = -l(l+1)\,Y . \tag{5}
$$

Its solutions are the spherical harmonics. Everything that follows is the work of solving (5).

### Separating the two angles

Separate again, $$Y(\theta,\varphi) = \Theta(\theta)\,\Phi(\varphi)$$. Substituting into (5) and multiplying by $$\sin^2\theta/(\Theta\Phi)$$ isolates $$\varphi$$:

$$
\frac{\sin\theta}{\Theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\right) + l(l+1)\sin^2\theta
\;=\; -\frac{1}{\Phi}\frac{d^2\Phi}{d\varphi^2} \;=\; m^2 . \tag{6}
$$

The azimuthal half is $$\Phi'' = -m^2\Phi$$, solved by $$e^{im\varphi}$$. Here the first integer appears, and notice where it comes from: not from the differential equation, which is happy with any $$m$$, but from the geometry. The directions $$\varphi$$ and $$\varphi + 2\pi$$ are the same direction, so $$\Phi$$ must be $$2\pi$$-periodic, and only integer $$m$$ is.

The polar half is the rest of (6). Multiplying through by $$\Theta/\sin^2\!\theta$$ puts it in the form

$$
\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{d}{d\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\frac{d\Theta}{d\theta}\right)
+ \left[l(l+1) - \frac{m^2}{\sin^2\!\theta}\right]\Theta = 0 .
$$

The awkwardness here is that $$\theta$$ never appears on its own, only through $$\sin\theta$$ and $$\cos\theta$$. That invites the substitution $$x = \cos\theta$$, writing $$P(x) = \Theta(\theta)$$. What is left is

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\!\left[(1-x^2)\frac{dP}{dx}\right] + \left[l(l+1) - \frac{m^2}{1-x^2}\right]P = 0 , \tag{7}
$$

which is the associated Legendre equation.

### Solving the Legendre equation

Take $$m = 0$$ first. Expanding the derivative in (7) gives the plain Legendre equation,

$$
(1-x^2)\,P'' - 2x\,P' + \ell(\ell+1)\,P = 0 ,
$$

whose coefficients are polynomials, and whose expansion point $$x = 0$$ is an ordinary point. That is the standard invitation to try a power series. Setting $$P = \sum_n a_n x^n$$, substituting, and collecting terms of equal power:

$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\Big[(n+2)(n+1)\,a_{n+2} - n(n-1)\,a_n - 2n\,a_n + \ell(\ell+1)\,a_n\Big]x^n = 0 .
$$

Every coefficient must vanish separately. Using $$\ell(\ell+1) - n(n+1) = (\ell-n)(\ell+n+1)$$, that is

$$
a_{n+2} = -\frac{(\ell-n)(\ell+n+1)}{(n+2)(n+1)}\, a_n .
$$

Notice the recurrence steps by two. So $$a_0$$ and $$a_1$$ are independent free constants: one generates a series in even powers, the other a series in odd powers, and those are the two linearly independent solutions the second-order equation must have.

Now the trouble. The ratio of successive coefficients tends to $$1$$,

$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\lvert \frac{a_{n+2}}{a_n} \right\rvert = 1 ,
$$

so each series converges inside $$\lvert x \rvert < 1$$ but diverges at $$x = \pm 1$$ — and those are $$\theta = 0$$ and $$\theta = \pi$$, the two poles, perfectly ordinary places on the sphere where nothing is allowed to blow up.

The factorised numerator is the way out. $$(\ell-n)(\ell+n+1)$$ vanishes at $$n = \ell$$, so if $$\ell$$ is a non-negative integer, the branch whose indices pass through $$\ell$$ stops dead at $$x^\ell$$ and becomes a polynomial of degree $$\ell$$ — the even series when $$\ell$$ is even, the odd one when $$\ell$$ is odd. The other branch is untouched and still diverges at the poles.

Fixing the leftover scale by $$P_\ell(1) = 1$$ names the survivors the Legendre polynomials:

$$
P_0 = 1 , \qquad P_1 = x , \qquad P_2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(3x^2-1) , \qquad P_3 = \tfrac{1}{2}(5x^3-3x) ,
$$

or in closed form, $$P_\ell(x) = \dfrac{1}{2^\ell \ell!}\dfrac{d^\ell}{dx^\ell}(x^2-1)^\ell$$.

It is worth being precise about what the boundary condition actually does. For integer $$\ell$$ every solution of (7) with $$m = 0$$ is a combination

$$
A\,P_\ell(x) + B\,Q_\ell(x) ,
$$

where $$Q_\ell$$ is the branch that never terminated — the Legendre function of the second kind, logarithmically infinite at the poles, as $$Q_0(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\frac{1+x}{1-x}$$ shows plainly. Requiring the solution to be bounded on $$[-1,1]$$ does not "terminate the series"; it sets $$B = 0$$ and keeps $$P_\ell$$.

For $$m \ne 0$$ the extra $$m^2/(1-x^2)$$ blows up at the same two points, and it can be absorbed by pulling out a factor that vanishes there. Writing $$P = (1-x^2)^{\lvert m \rvert/2} u$$ leaves an equation for $$u$$ of the same kind, whose series again terminates only for integer $$l$$ — and now only when $$l - \lvert m \rvert \ge 0$$. Hence $$\lvert m \rvert \le l$$, and the solutions are

$$
P_l^m(x) = (-1)^m (1-x^2)^{m/2}\,\frac{d^m}{dx^m} P_l(x) ,
$$

the associated Legendre functions. Both integers have now appeared, and neither came out of a differential equation: $$m$$ because a direction repeats after $$2\pi$$, $$l$$ because a potential cannot be infinite at the poles. The first few, with $$x = \cos\theta$$:

$$
P_0^0 = 1 , \qquad P_1^0 = x , \qquad P_1^1 = -\sqrt{1-x^2} , \qquad
P_2^0 = \tfrac{1}{2}(3x^2-1) , \qquad P_2^1 = -3x\sqrt{1-x^2} .
$$

### What survives

Fixing the scale so they are orthonormal over the sphere:

$$
Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi) = \sqrt{\frac{2l+1}{4\pi}\,\frac{(l-m)!}{(l+m)!}}\;\; P_l^m(\cos\theta)\;e^{im\varphi} ,
\qquad l = 0,1,2,\dots , \quad -l \le m \le l . \tag{8}
$$

There are $$2l+1$$ of them at each $$l$$. The first few:

$$
Y_0^0 = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi}} , \qquad
Y_1^0 = \sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}\cos\theta , \qquad
Y_1^{1} = -\sqrt{\frac{3}{8\pi}}\,\sin\theta\,e^{i\varphi} , \qquad
Y_2^0 = \sqrt{\frac{5}{16\pi}}\,(3\cos^2\theta - 1) .
$$

Reassembling with the radial factor (4), every solution of Laplace's equation is a sum of these:

$$
f(r,\theta,\varphi) = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=-l}^{l}
\left(A_{lm}\,r^{\,l} + \frac{B_{lm}}{r^{\,l+1}}\right) Y_l^m(\theta,\varphi) . \tag{9}
$$

Two things worth noticing on the way out. The combinations $$r^l Y_l^m$$ are the *solid harmonics*, and each is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $$l$$ in $$x, y, z$$ — $$r Y_1^0$$ is just $$z$$ up to its constant. And because (5) is an eigenvalue equation for a self-adjoint operator, its eigenfunctions are orthogonal and complete: any reasonable function on the sphere expands in them, which is why they turn up far from electrostatics, wherever something has to be written down as a function of direction.
